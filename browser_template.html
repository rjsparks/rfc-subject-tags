RFC Subject Tags

    The hierarchy

    Roots are tags too. The eighteen top-level subjects head the tree for browsing, and a document about a subject in general carries the root alone. A tag applied to an RFC implies its whole path, so a parent's count includes its children; the root row's bar is the whole subject.

    Topics are subjects and cross-cutting aspects, set in violet in the text face; technologies are named protocols, systems and formats, set in the RFC series' own typewriter face. Roots are always topics. Search matches tag names, aliases, covers and descriptions on whole words. An alias is another name for the tag; covers names something the tag stands in for because no more specific tag exists. Two measures, kept apart: Corpus is how many RFCs carry a tag; mail/yr is how many notifications a subscriber would have received per year since January 2021. When they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

    technology topic corpus count notifications per year

      Selection

      Pick a tag on the left. You will get its place in the tree, its kind and definition, the tags it travels with, and the RFCs themselves by number and title.

      Tags that travel together

      Every pair of tags directly assigned to the same RFC. A pair near the top that also sounds like one idea is a merge candidate; a tag that appears only inside pairs never stands on its own. Pairs where one tag is the other's ancestor are marked kin: the tree already states that relationship. Raise the threshold to cut the tail.

        When each tag was in use

        First to last RFC carrying the tag, across 1968–2026, newest last use first. Blue means the tag has been used since 2021 and would still notify someone; rust means nothing has carried it in over a decade; grey is in between. A vocabulary for subscription should be mostly blue, and the rust rows are the ones to weigh: a finished protocol still deserves a search tag, but not a subscription.

          Tags assigned to the same RFCs

          Two different measures, because they mean different things. Two-way overlap (Jaccard, shared ÷ combined) high in both directions says two tags are doing one job — a redundancy candidate. One-way overlap (containment) high in one direction and low in the other says one tag sits inside the other — a broader/narrower candidate the tree does not yet record. Pairs already related as ancestor and descendant are excluded.

          A third possibility is worth naming, because it looks like the second. High containment can mean the tags are genuinely nested, or it can mean a match rule in taxonomy.yaml is over-firing. Read the shared RFCs before concluding the vocabulary is at fault — the fix may belong in the rule table instead.
